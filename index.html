Stop-and-Frisk in New York City, 2022

Question 1

Demographic analysis

What is the racial and gender distribution of individuals who were stopped and frisked? Are there disparities by race or gender?

Black New Yorkers made up 58.7% of stops (8,863) while being 20.2% of the city's population. Hispanic people were 29.6% of stops (28.3% of residents), White people 7.1% (30.9% of residents) and Asian / Pacific Islander people 2.1% (15.6% of residents).

  • Per 100,000 residents, Black New Yorkers were stopped about 499 times, Hispanic about 180, White about 40 and Asian about 23. That makes the Black stop rate roughly 12.6× the White rate and the Hispanic rate roughly 4.5×.
  • Men were 91.6% of stops (13,829) and women 7.5% (1,139). Black men alone were 54.5% of all stops.
  • Disparities in who gets stopped are large by race and extreme by gender (women are roughly half the city's residents but 7.5% of stops).

Question 2

Temporal trends

How did the number of stops change over the months of 2022? Are there specific days of the week or times of day when stops are more common?

  • Months: stops climbed from about 1,150 in January–February to a spring peak in May (1,471), with March–May accounting for 28.9% of the year. Volume then drifted down through the summer and fall and hit the year's low in December (841), about 33% below the monthly average of 1,259.
  • Days: Wednesday (2,537) was the busiest day and Monday (1,561) the quietest, so Wednesdays had about 63% more stops. Saturday and Thursday were also high. Adjusting for how many of each weekday there were in 2022 (Saturdays occurred 53 times) doesn't change the picture.
  • Time of day: stops are strongly concentrated at night. 42% happened between 6 pm and midnight and another 17% between midnight and 3 am. The peak hour was 10–11 pm (1,207); the quietest was 6–7 am (132).

Question 3

Reasons for stops

What are the most common reasons cited by officers? Is there a relationship between the stated reason and the outcome?

  • The suspected crime officers cite is overwhelmingly criminal possession of a weapon (CPW): 6,908 stops, 45.7%. Next are robbery (10.2%), petit larceny (8.8%), assault (8.4%) and burglary (7.1%).
  • Yes, the reason strongly predicts the outcome. Petit-larceny stops end in arrest 61% of the time, grand larceny 51% and robbery 46%, but CPW stops end in arrest only 21% of the time and in no arrest or summons 76% of the time.
  • Weapon stops are the ones most likely to involve a frisk (87%) and the most likely to turn up a weapon (24%), while property-crime stops are searched more but rarely find weapons (petit larceny: 3.9%).
  • Who started the stop matters too. Self-initiated stops (35% of stops) end in arrest just 23% of the time versus 39% for stops from a radio run.

Question 4

Outcomes of stops

What percentage of stops result in arrests, summonses, or other outcomes? Are there differences by demographics?

  • 33.0% of stops ended in an arrest (4,985) and 2.8% in a summons (420). 64.6% (9,750 stops) ended with neither, meaning most people stopped were let go without being charged.
  • Other outcomes: 60.0% of people were frisked and 42.2% were searched.
  • By race the arrest rate is nearly flat (Black 32.9%, Hispanic 32.5%, Asian/PI 35.3%), with White people somewhat higher at 37.7%. But the frisk rate differs a lot: 63.5% of Black and 60.6% of Hispanic people were frisked versus 33.2% of White people.
  • Women were arrested more often than men (36.4% vs 32.9%) but frisked far less (35.5% vs 61.9%).
  • Outcomes differ more by borough than by race: 72.6% of Bronx stops ended with no arrest or summons vs 58% in Manhattan.

Question 5

Location analysis

Which neighborhoods or precincts have the most stops? Is there a correlation between where stops happen and the demographics of the population?

  • The Bronx (4,495) and Brooklyn (4,465) together account for 59% of stops. Relative to population the Bronx is far ahead at 305 stops per 100,000 residents, versus 198 in Manhattan, 163 in Brooklyn, 98 in Queens and 89 on Staten Island.
  • The single busiest precinct is the 46th (University Heights / Morris Heights, Bronx) with 1,143 stops, 7.6% of the whole city's total, more than twice the next-highest (75th, East New York, 502). The top 10 precincts account for 31.7% of stops, and 7 of the top 10 are in the Bronx.
  • Correlation with demographics: at the borough level, the share of residents who are Black or Hispanic is strongly correlated with the stop rate (r = 0.85, n = 5). Across precincts, the places with more stops tend to be those where a larger share of the people stopped were Black or Hispanic (r = 0.39) and a smaller share were White (r = −0.38). Because the stop file contains no precinct population data these precinct figures describe the people stopped, not the residents.

Question 6

Weapon and contraband discovery

How frequently do officers find weapons or contraband? Is there a connection between discovery and the reason for the stop?

  • A weapon was found in 16.0% of stops (2,411) and other contraband in 5.8% (875). Combined, 20.0% of stops (3,020) turned up a weapon or contraband, so in four of five stops nothing was found.
  • Among people who were frisked or searched (11,315), 25.4% had a weapon or contraband. Frisks alone found a weapon 21.0% of the time and searches 30.6%.
  • Yes, discovery is tied to the reason: weapon-related stops yield weapons far more often (CPW 24.4%, menacing 25.4%, robbery 15.6%) than property-crime stops (burglary 4.5%, petit larceny 3.9%). Contraband runs the other way, found most in petit larceny (11.8%) and burglary (8.4%) stops.
  • Weapon discovery is highest when officers cite actions suggesting a concealed weapon (30.8%), compared with about 12% when the stop was based on matching a description or being near a crime scene.

Question 7

Repeat encounters

How many individuals were stopped and frisked multiple times in 2022? Are there patterns in the demographics or outcomes?

This question can't be answered with this file. Each of the 15,102 rows is one stop with a unique STOP_ID. The public file has no name, ID, date of birth or any other identifier for the person, so there is no way to tell whether two rows are the same individual. Guessing by matching on age, sex, race and location would produce false matches, so no “repeat” count is reported here.

What the data can say about multiple people in the same stop: in 4,630 stops (30.7%) the officer recorded that other people were also stopped in the same encounter. That is a different thing from a person being stopped repeatedly, but it shows that the 15,102 stops involve more than 15,102 people.

To study repeat encounters you would need person-level linked data (for example through a records request or a research data agreement) and could then compare demographics and outcomes between people stopped once and people stopped more than once.

Question 8

Age and stop frequency

Is there a relationship between age and the likelihood of being stopped? How do the reasons differ across age groups?

  • Stops skew young: median age 26 (mean 29.3). 13.0% were under 18 and 38.1% under 25. The single most common ages were 20 (821), 30 (711), 25 (702) and 18 (693). Age was missing for 1,723 stops (11.4%), and percentages here use the stops with an age.
  • The file has no age-group population counts, so a true per-capita “likelihood” by age can't be computed here. The counts do show that stops are concentrated among teens and people in their 20s (about half of stops with a known age are of people under 28).
  • Reasons change with age: CPW is the cited reason in 61% of stops of 18–20-year-olds but only 20% for people 50+. Robbery falls from 21% (≤15) to 7–8% (40+), whereas petit larceny rises from 2% (18–20) to 22% (50+) and burglary from 2–4% (under 21) to 13%.
  • Outcomes also change: stops of 16–24-year-olds found a weapon about 22% of the time vs 10% for 50+, while arrests rise with age (30% at 18–20 to 44% at 50+).

Question 9

Officer identification

Which officers have conducted the most stop-and-frisk encounters? Are there variations in outcomes based on the officers involved?

Individual officers are not identifiable in this file. The public data includes the issuing officer's rank and command code (the precinct or unit they work for) but no name, tax ID or badge number. So the analysis below is at the command level, not the individual-officer level.
  • There are 199 different commands. Command 46 issued the most stops (1,234, 8.2%), followed by command 75 (406), 40 (363), 52 (360) and 44 (296). The top 10 commands account for 26.2% of all stops. 77% of stops were issued by the command that covers the precinct where the stop happened.
  • Outcomes vary enormously by command. Among the 63 commands with at least 100 stops, the arrest rate ranges from 11.3% to 67.6%, the weapon-found rate from 3.7% to 38.4%, and the share of stops ending with no arrest or summons from 31.5% to 86.1%. The busiest command (46) also had one of the lowest arrest rates (11.3%) with 86.1% of stops ending in no arrest or summons.
  • By rank, 12,383 stops (82%) were issued by officers with the rank code POM and 1,615 (10.7%) by POF (the NYPD's legacy male/female police-officer codes). Arrest rates are similar across ranks (about 28–35%), while weapon-found rates are higher for sergeants (20.5%) and detectives (DTS 19.7%) than for POF (12.9%), though these groups are small.

Question 10

Comparative analysis

How does 2022 compare to previous years in overall numbers and demographic distribution? Have practices or outcomes changed over time?

  • Compared with the 2011 peak (685,724 stops), 2022's 15,102 stops are just 2.2%. It's about 8% of 2013's volume (191,851), the year of the federal court ruling that the NYPD's practices were unconstitutional.
  • But 2022 was a sharp rebound from recent lows: +69% over 2021 (8,947) and the highest count since 2015 (22,565). The upward trend continued afterwards: 16,971 stops in 2023 and 25,386 in 2024.
  • The racial makeup has barely changed. Black people were between 48% and 60% of all stops in every year since 2003 and have been 56–60% since 2017 (58.7% in 2022). The Latinx share has stayed near 28–33% (33% in 2011, 29.6% in 2022), and the White share has fallen from about 9–11% to 7.1% in 2022 and 5–6% in 2023–24.
  • Outcomes: the share of stops with no arrest or summons rose from 60.6% in 2021 to 64.6% in 2022, and for weapons-related stops from 71.3% to 75.8% (per the Data Collaborative for Justice analysis of NYPD data). So the NYPD's yield, in terms of arrests, was lower in 2022 than in 2021.

Data & method

Data: NYPD Stop, Question and Frisk 2022 file (15,102 records, one per stop). All 2022 figures were computed directly from this file. Percentages are of all 15,102 stops unless noted. “Hispanic” = White Hispanic + Black Hispanic. “No arrest or summons” = neither flag was marked. “Weapon found” and “contraband found” use the file's WEAPON_FOUND_FLAG and OTHER_CONTRABAND_FLAG.

Limitations

  • The file counts stops, not unique people, and has no individual or officer identifiers (questions 7 and 9).
  • Race, sex and age are recorded by the officer; 192 stops have no race, 134 no sex and 1,723 no age.
  • Differences between groups are descriptive. They don't by themselves show the cause of a disparity (for example, differences in where police are deployed, or in who is out in public at night).
  • Population-based rates use 2020 Census counts of residents, not the people actually present in an area.
